Merger savings, checked line by line

Mergers promise savings. Tie-Out shows which ones are real.

When two companies combine, they can buy their supplies together and pay less. The quick estimate of that saving is almost always too big. Tie-Out checks it against both companies’ purchase records and keeps only what holds up.

01

Two companies, one shopping list.

Both companies buy screws, tape, valves, gloves … often the very same items, sometimes from the same suppliers, at different prices.

Merge them and the combined company can ask every supplier for the better price. That price gap, added up across everything they both buy, is the saving everyone wants to count.

02

But their records don’t agree.

Each company typed its records its own way: different spellings, shorthand, units and pack sizes. To find what they really buy in common, every pair has to be tied out: checked word by word. Try it on three real records from this sample.

    Under the hood

    For the technically curious.

    The same story in the precise terms: what the engine does to the two purchasing extracts, how it is measured and what it cannot see.

    1. 1

      Ingest

      Two CSV extracts, column mapping and validation; currencies converted at stated rates; rows that cannot be priced are flagged, not dropped.

    2. 2

      Normalise

      Descriptions upper-cased, abbreviations expanded from a reference list, inch and dimension marks standardised; units converted to a base unit, pack sizes parsed from text.

    3. 3

      Match vendors

      Tax-ID merge, exact normalised names (legal forms and punctuation stripped), then fuzzy candidates scored by token sort and Jaro-Winkler; a parent table links subsidiaries.

    4. 4

      Match items

      Tier A: the same manufacturer part number. Tier B: character n-gram TF-IDF similarity above a threshold with critical attributes agreeing and none in conflict. Tier C: review only, never counted.

    5. 5

      Price groups

      Per item, company, site and parent vendor: a volume-weighted base-unit price. The benchmark is the lowest price among groups with real volume and repeat purchases.

    6. 6

      Synergy levers

      Price harmonisation (the bridge), volume consolidation by category, and payment terms as one-time cash, never added to the yearly figure.

    7. 7

      Risk

      Concentration caps and sole-source flags, contract end dates, phasing with ramp-up, and one-time costs of switching.

    8. 8

      Exports

      A formula-driven workbook with a tie-out check, an eight-slide deck and chart images. Every figure traces to source line IDs.

    The accuracy scorecard, in full
    Held-out accuracy, Tie-Out against the naive baseline
    MeasureTie-OutNaiveTarget

    Held-out seed 4, never used for tuning. The naive design matches vendors by fuzzy names and items by word-level text similarity, with no attribute checks. False-savings rate is dollar-weighted: savings resting on wrong matches, divided by all savings reported. Targets are acceptance criteria, not claims.

    The seven bridge steps, precisely
    The naive figure
    For every matched item (including review-only matches), each buying group’s price minus the lowest price paid anywhere, times its yearly quantity, at recorded pack units and counting gaps inside one company.
    1. Unit-of-measure errors
    Suspected unit errors and pack units that cannot be converted.
    2. Unverified matches
    Tier C pairs, moved to “upside pending review”.
    3. Spot-price benchmark
    The difference between the absolute minimum price and the benchmark price paid on at least a tenth of the volume over three or more lines.
    4. Internal variance
    Gaps against a benchmark held by the same company: available without the deal.
    5. Non-comparable terms
    Pairs whose delivery terms (incoterms) differ, held for review.
    6. Contract lock-in
    Gap on volume under contract beyond the planning horizon.
    7. Capture probability
    The share of the remaining gap not expected to be won in negotiation.

    Step i is the figure with filters 1 to i−1 on, minus the figure with filters 1 to i on, so each dollar is attributed to the first step that removes it.

    What this analysis cannot see
    • Rebates, credits and off-invoice discounts
    • Specification, quality and lead-time differences between apparently identical items
    • Freight and delivery terms, unless incoterms are supplied
    • Contract clauses such as minimum volumes, exclusivity and change of control
    • Index-linked prices caught at different dates
    • Supplier capacity and willingness to serve the combined volume
    • Services and other spend with neither an item code nor a part number, beyond vendor and category totals
    • Corporate parent links not supplied in the parent table

    About the data and this project

    All data is synthetic and illustrative. The companies, their suppliers, items and prices are generated; no real company or supplier is represented.

    This workflow is not new. Consulting firms and spend-analytics vendors do it with proprietary tooling. Tie-Out is an open implementation in which every matching rule, every formula and every dollar can be audited. It is not legal advice, and its masked view is a presentation mode, not an access control.
